The 4.2 Is the New 4.8

In Paris, the city’s best tables often seem to live in the low fours. The closer a restaurant creeps toward perfect, the more carefully you should inspect the evidence.

By The Ratings DeskParis — Sept. 29, 2026An unscientific field theory

There is a particular moment in the life of a new Paris resident when Google Maps stops being a map and becomes a trap.

You are hungry. You are somewhere between République and Oberkampf. You search “restaurant,” sort mentally for anything above 4.6, and walk past a packed little place with a 4.2 because the internet has trained you to believe that 4.2 means “probably fine.” Twenty minutes later you are eating a €19 burrata beneath a neon sign in a restaurant rated 4.9.

This is when the theory begins to form.

In many cities, restaurant ratings behave roughly the way you expect: 4.8 is better than 4.4, which is better than 4.1. Paris is more mischievous. Here, the curve can feel almost flat through the low fours, then strangely inverted at the top. A 4.1 or 4.2 can be extraordinary. A 4.9 can be a warning to read the reviews very carefully.

This is not a law, and it is certainly not science. Paris contains superb 4.9s and deeply mediocre 4.1s. But as a piece of street-level heuristics, the low-four phenomenon is remarkably useful.

The rating is not only rating the food

A restaurant review is supposed to answer a simple question: was the restaurant good?

In practice it answers a much messier one: did the restaurant behave the way I expected a restaurant to behave?

Paris introduces friction into that equation. Tables are close together. Kitchens close. Reservations matter. A waiter may not perform warmth in the American register. Your water may not arrive before you ask. The restaurant may be unwilling to turn one dish into another dish. Dinner can take two hours because dinner takes two hours.

None of these things necessarily make the restaurant bad. But they are exceptionally good at producing three-star reviews.

The result is that certain very Parisian restaurants are being judged partly on the quality of the food and partly on whether they successfully translated themselves for someone arriving with a different set of expectations.

Tourists review. Regulars return.

The second distortion is simpler.

People who find restaurants through Google are also more likely to review restaurants on Google. Visitors are disproportionately dependent on ratings, maps and English-language search. They arrive through the platform and leave through the platform.

This creates a strange asymmetry. A restaurant that is exceptionally good at acquiring tourists can also become exceptionally good at accumulating reviews. Friendly English-language service, photogenic plating, all-day hours, frictionless walk-ins and a gentle reminder to “leave us five stars” are all perfectly reasonable business decisions. They are also decisions that can push a restaurant toward 4.8 or 4.9 without necessarily pushing the cooking into the same percentile.

Meanwhile, the place next door may have 386 reviews, a 4.2, a menu written in chalk and no apparent interest in whether the internet understands it.

Which one do you want?

Exactly. Now you are thinking like a Parisian conspiracy theorist.

Great restaurants generate specific complaints

One of the peculiarities of excellent restaurants is that they often have opinions.

They serve a narrow menu. They run out of things. They do not seat incomplete parties. They may tell you the table is needed back at 9:30. They may refuse substitutions because the dish is the dish. They may be tiny, loud, dark, slow, crowded, difficult to reserve and inconvenient for six people who have just decided they would like dinner.

These are operational constraints, but they also generate reviews.

Read the one-star section of a beloved Paris restaurant and you will often find a parallel newspaper devoted entirely to grievances: “would not change the sauce,” “too close to the next table,” “waiter seemed annoyed,” “no table without reservation,” “kitchen closed at 2:15.”

The trick is not to ignore bad reviews. It is to classify them.

A review that says “the fish was badly overcooked” is about the restaurant. A review that says “they would not make carbonara for my child” may be about something else.

The danger zone of perfection

A 4.9 is not inherently suspicious. But perfection deserves context.

How many reviews are there? How old is the restaurant? Are the comments oddly repetitive? Are most reviewers visitors? Does the restaurant appear to have been engineered for broad approval, or does it have a point of view?

High ratings are most useful when they are difficult to obtain. Once every pleasant place becomes a 4.7, the scale loses resolution. A 4.9 then tells you less than it appears to tell you.

The number looks precise. The meaning is fuzzy.

Paris simply makes that fuzziness unusually visible.

The 4.2 sweet spot

The interesting territory is roughly 4.1 to 4.4.

Here you find places that have accumulated enough friction to lose the halo but not enough failure to collapse. They may be old. They may be busy. They may have been reviewed by enough people with enough different expectations that the score has settled into something more revealing than universal approval.

A 4.2 says: there is evidence here.

Your job is to read it.

Look at the newest reviews, not only the “most relevant.” Look at the one-stars and ask what people are actually angry about. Look for recurring complaints about food, cleanliness or value; those matter. Discount complaints that amount to “Paris happened to me.”

“Discount complaints that amount to: Paris happened to me.”

Then look at the photos taken by customers, preferably the ugly ones.

If the room is full, the menu is short, the food looks serious and the negative reviews are mostly about attitude, timing or the existential size of a French table, you may have found dinner.

A better rule than stars

The point is not to reverse the rating system and declare bad ratings good. That would be stupid.

The point is to stop pretending the number is the restaurant.

A rating is an aggregate of food, expectations, cultural translation, customer mix, review solicitation, price sensitivity, operational quirks and the mood of someone whose entrée arrived twelve minutes later than anticipated.

In Paris, those variables are loud enough that the score itself becomes a kind of cultural document.

So yes: the next time you are choosing between a 4.2 and a 4.9, resist the reflex.

Open both.

Read the complaints.

Then, possibly, choose the 4.2.

Editor’s note
This is a field theory, not a dataset. Paris contains excellent 4.9s, mediocre 4.1s and every possible contradiction in between. The proposed curve is a joke with practical ambitions.
